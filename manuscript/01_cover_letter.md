We are pleased to submit our manuscript, **"Cost and non-financial barriers to
healthcare in South Korea, 2020–2023: a four-wave latent transition analysis"**, for
consideration as a **Full-length article** in *Health Policy*.

Most of what health systems know about unmet healthcare need comes from annual
prevalence figures. Those figures answer how many people went without care they
believed they needed, but not the question policy-makers actually face: whether the
same people keep going without it. A country can show a falling headline rate while a
minority is repeatedly shut out, and an expansion of financial protection can look
successful on the headline rate while leaving the people whose barrier is not money
exactly where they were.

We address that question in South Korea, a single-payer universal coverage system that
combines near-universal enrolment with unusually high cost sharing and a dental
benefit package that covers very little. Following a nationally representative cohort
of adults through four annual waves of the Korea Health Panel (2020–2023), we
distinguish forgone physician and dental care by whether the stated barrier was cost
or something other than cost, and we estimate a latent transition model with
measurement invariance across waves. This lets us report not only how common each kind
of barrier is, but how long it lasts and whether people move between kinds.

Three findings seem to us directly relevant to an international policy readership.
First, a four-status typology fits the data: no unmet need, a cost-driven status
concentrated in dental care, a non-financially constrained status, and a small
compound status in which both barriers operate in both domains. Second, the two barrier
types are close to non-interchangeable from one year to the next: people rarely move
between them, so they behave as distinct and durable conditions rather than as
different expressions of a single underlying difficulty. Third, the cost-driven status
is the more persistent of the two despite being the less prevalent, and the compound
status almost never returns directly to having no unmet need. The policy reading is
that a coverage expansion and a service-organisation reform are not substitutes, and
that the groups each would reach are largely different people.

The work speaks to a question *Health Policy* has published on before — Ko's panel
analysis of unmet need and health status in this journal (2016;120:646–53) established
that unmet need in Korea is more than a reporting artefact — and it extends that
literature methodologically by moving from "who reports unmet need" to "who stays
without care, and for which reason". The implications generalise to any system where
financial protection and service availability are governed by separate policy
instruments.
